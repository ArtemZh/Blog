Past tenses in French

English gets by with “I did”, “I was doing” and “I had done”. French splits the past more finely, and the choice between two tenses — passé composé and imparfait — is where most learners stumble. This page shows which tense does which job, how each one is built, and ends with a test.

01Five past tenses at a glance

Four are used every day, in speech and in writing. The fifth, together with its companion, lives only in books.

completed eventPassé composé

J'ai mangé une pomme.

I ate an apple / I have eaten an apple. The workhorse of spoken French: things that happened and are done.

background, habit, stateImparfait

Il pleuvait. Je lisais tous les soirs.

It was raining. I used to read every evening. The scenery and the routines around the events.

the past before the pastPlus-que-parfait

J'avais oublié mes clés.

I had forgotten my keys. Something that happened before another past moment.

just happenedPassé récent

Je viens de finir.

I have just finished. Built with venir de + infinitive.

books onlyPassé simple · passé antérieur

Il entra. Quand il eut fini, il sortit.

He came in. When he had finished, he went out. Written narrative; in speech they become passé composé and plus-que-parfait.

Trap. Mapping English tenses one to one. “I was” is sometimes j'étais (a state) and sometimes j'ai été (a completed episode: j'ai été malade pendant deux jours). The question is never “which English tense is this?” but “is this an event or the background?”.

    05Imparfait: one rule, one exception

    Take the nous form of the present tense, drop -ons, add -ais, -ais, -ait, -ions, -iez, -aient. The only verb that does not follow this is être, whose stem is ét-.

    Spelling keeps the sound: verbs in -ger keep an e before a (je mangeais but nous mangions), verbs in -cer take ç before a (je commençais but nous commencions).

    What the imparfait is for

    Description and scenery

    La maison était grande et il faisait froid.

    Habits and repeated actions

    Le dimanche, nous allions chez ma grand-mère.

    Something in progress

    Je regardais la télé quand tu as appelé.

    States: age, time, weather, feelings

    J'avais dix ans. Il était midi. J'étais content.

    06Imparfait or passé composé?

    Think of a film. The imparfait is the set, the light and what the characters were doing when the camera started. The passé composé is what happens in the scene — the actions that move the story forward.

    ImparfaitPassé composé
    Question it answersWhat was it like? What was going on? What used to happen?What happened? What happened next?
    Shape in timeopen, no clear start or endclosed, a finished whole
    Typical signal wordsautrefois, d'habitude, souvent, toujours, tous les jours, le lundi, pendant que, quand j'étais petithier, soudain, tout à coup, un jour, une fois, ce matin, à ce moment-là, trois fois, pendant deux heures
    TogetherJe dormais (background) quand le téléphone a sonné (event).

    Trap. “Long action means imparfait.” Duration alone does not decide. J'ai habité à Lyon pendant dix ans is passé composé: the ten years are presented as a finished, closed block. Signal words help, but the real question is whether the speaker shows the action as a whole or as the background to something else.

    07Before the past, and just now

    plus-que-parfaitImparfait of avoir / être + participle

    Quand je suis arrivé, le film avait déjà commencé.

    When I arrived, the film had already started. The same choice of auxiliary and the same agreement rules as the passé composé: elle était partie. It also follows si for regrets: Si j'avais su, je serais venu — if I had known, I would have come.

    passé récentvenir de + infinitive

    Je viens de rentrer. Ils viennent d'arriver.

    I have just come home. They have just arrived. Put venir in the imparfait to say “had just” in a past story: il venait de partir — he had just left.

    Trap. Translating “I have just…” with the passé composé and juste. J'ai juste mangé means “I only ate”. For “just now”, French uses venir de.

    08Passé simple and passé antérieur: recognise, rarely produce

    In novels, fairy tales, history books and newspaper narratives, events are told in the passé simple; an event that came just before another one is in the passé antérieur (passé simple of the auxiliary + participle). In conversation and in most everyday writing, they are replaced by the passé composé and the plus-que-parfait. For a learner the goal is to recognise the forms when reading.

    Verb typeil / elleils / ellesExample
    -er verbs-a-èrentparler → il parla, ils parlèrent
    -ir and most -re verbs-it-irentfinir → il finit, ils finirent; prendre → il prit
    many -oir verbs and others-ut-urentvouloir → il voulut; lire → il lut
    the ones to know by sightêtre → il fut · avoir → il eut · faire → il fit · venir → il vint · voir → il vit · aller → il alla · naître → il naquit · mourir → il mourut

    Trap. Il finit can be the present or the passé simple — the forms are identical for many -ir verbs in the singular. In a story told in the past, it is the passé simple.
