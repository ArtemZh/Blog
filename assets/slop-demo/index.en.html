Analysis · August 2026

Where project management is heading.

Six shifts that by 2026 stopped being a forecast and became a manager’s daily work. And separately — what gets talked about less: how, along with them, the question of what exactly a manager is accountable for is changing.

Every year someone publishes a list of trends, and every time half of the items turn out to be the same item reworded. So I will start not with a list but with a distinction. Some shifts change the tools: a new button appears, you learn to press it, and six months later you no longer remember how it was before. Other shifts change the questions a manager is obliged to answer. The first cost a week of training. The second rewrite what you will be held accountable for.

2026 is interesting because both types met on one topic. AI entered project work first as a tool — to prepare minutes, collect a status, sketch a work breakdown. And then, almost unnoticed, it raised a question this profession did not have before: if part of the work is done by something other than a person, who is accountable for the result of that work. And that is no longer a question about a tool.

Below are six directions on a chart, four stages teams go through in this transition, and three analyses from PMI material where this same accountability is broken down into concrete mechanisms: the responsibility matrix, the work breakdown structure and the line beyond which the decision stays with a person.

Chart one

What pushes hardest.

Six directions are laid out on two axes. Horizontally — how far the shift has already arrived: on the left, what has become everyday and does not ask for your consent; on the right, what is still forming and is so far discussed at conferences. Vertically — how strongly it changes the manager’s work itself, not just the set of tools on their desk.

The split is not academic. The bottom-right quarter is what you can put off and come back to in a year. The top-left is what you are already dealing with, even if you have not named it out loud. Most discussions about trends confuse these two quarters and end with nothing because of it.

The most interesting thing on this chart is not the AI dot itself but what has gathered around it. Paying for outcomes instead of hours turns an estimate from a forecast into a commitment: if you got the estimate wrong, it is no longer “we guessed wrong” but an unfulfilled contract. Hybrid teams remove the synchronous meeting as the backbone of the process — when half the team is in another time zone and some join for two months as contractors, status has to be collected differently. Skills instead of job titles break the org chart as a description of reality.

None of these shifts needs AI to happen. But each of them makes manual information gathering more expensive — and that is exactly why they arrived together.

Chart two

Four stages of maturity.

Teams do not move to AI-assisted management in one leap. Between “we tried it” and “we can’t work without it” lie four stages, and they are not about the number of licenses but about what has changed in the work.

Almost everyone gets stuck at the second. It looks like this: routine really has been automated, minutes and statuses are no longer typed by hand, time has been freed up — and there it all stopped. The freed time never made it into the plan, estimates stayed the same, meetings run the same way. Formally the rollout happened. In practice nothing changed except that people type less.

Moving to the third stage costs more than any rollout, because it requires changing a habit, not a tool: estimating durations from completed projects rather than gut feeling, and checking a number rather than accepting it because the system produced it.

Analysis · PMI

Who is accountable when an agent does part of the work. 1

At the end of July PMI published an analysis of what happens to the RACI matrix when some of the doers are not people. Any manager can sketch this matrix from memory: Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed, and the main rule — exactly one Accountable per outcome. The grid was never the hard part. The hard part starts on a live project, where priorities shift and people feel left out the minute they are not invited to a decision that matters to them.

The question of “who owns what” gets sharper when part of the team is not human. AI is already an ordinary part of project work: it summarizes meetings, drafts statuses, analyzes risks, finds themes in stakeholder feedback. Increasingly, agents also take over bounded pieces of a process entirely. The principles of stakeholder engagement do not change — how they are applied does.

Three questions that did not exist before

  • Who reviews what was generated?A status draft or minutes are still someone’s text, and someone is accountable for what it says.
  • Who approves the action?An agent can prepare a decision, but the button must be pressed by whoever will be asked about it later.
  • Who owns the escalation?If an AI-driven process produced a wrong result, there must be one name in the Accountable column — that rule has not moved.

PMI calls the nearest shift not autonomous delivery but augmented intelligence: AI does not replace a person but helps them do their work better. It prepares faster, spots patterns earlier, communicates more evenly — but context, judgment, understanding of stakeholders and final accountability stay with the manager.

There is also a point that is not about AI at all but explains why this whole conversation about roles is not bureaucracy. From PMI research on project success: a project can hit every deadline and stay within budget — and still be considered a failure if those whose opinion matters did not see value in it. Roles, relationships and clarity about who owns what are part of that judgment on a par with the schedule.

What you can apply tomorrow

If two functions claim the Accountable column — or both avoid it — a neater grid will not help. That is a decision the sponsor usually has to make. Force that conversation to happen in advance, before an incident forces it.

Chart three · PMI

What gets added to the work plan. 2

The most expensive work on a project is the work nobody planned. It shows up late and as a surprise: an integration that was nobody’s task, a verification step everyone thought belonged to someone else, a deliverable that fell into the gap between two teams. When it finally surfaces, fixing it costs many times more than anticipating it would have.

A work breakdown structure exists exactly to catch such work early. Sketching one today takes seconds: a template in the tracker, or AI producing a first pass from a short brief before you finish your coffee. The hard part is still elsewhere: deciding what is in the project and what is not, and where one piece of work ends and the next begins. The same discipline now also answers a new question — what exactly the agent will do and what it will not.

The example in the analysis is mundane: an assistant that drafts replies to customer requests. Built as a regular product feature, it gives the expected structure — requirements, development, testing, rollout, management. Built as an agentic process, it keeps all these branches and adds five new ones, and what adds them is not the technology but the delegated freedom to act.

The principle does not change. The structure still describes deliverables, not activities, and the 100% rule still applies: everything within the project scope and nothing extra. What expands is not the form but the content — it now includes the agent’s behavior and the control system around it.

An old formula that works again

If it is not in the WBS, it is not in the project. Work that cannot be found in the structure has to be approved first and done afterwards. For twenty years this phrase was about protecting against scope creep. Now it also describes the boundary of an agent’s authority.

Analysis · PMI

Automation, assistance, augmentation. 3

The question of “where exactly a person is needed” is usually settled by intuition, and so anew each time. PMI suggests breaking it down along two dimensions. The first is task complexity: how many variables it has, whether it needs business context, whether it needs actual project management experience. The second is how much a person has to intervene to get a usable result. The relationship is direct: the more complex the task, the more intervention — and this field shows which mode of work you have actually chosen.

  • AutomationThe task is simple and repetitive. A person sets the rule once and spot-checks.
  • AssistanceThe tool prepares a draft; a person reviews it and brings it to a usable state.
  • AugmentationThe decision stays with a person; the tool only widens what they can cover.

    «AI is excellent at managing data… It understands the how and what. Humans understand the why.»

    That is Juanita Woods, PMP, PgMP, at PMI Global Summit in Atlanta. She also offered a comparison that explains the need for oversight better than any manual: a tool with the intelligence of a savant and the emotional age of a five-year-old. You can and should work with it; you should not leave it unsupervised.

    The reason a person cannot be taken out of the loop is put briefly in the analysis. An algorithm will not push back on leadership, will not defend an ethical line and will not take responsibility for its own choice. A manager can do all of that — and that is exactly why a project with generative AI needs a person in the loop, not around it.

    What follows from this.

    The manager’s role is shifting from the person who collects information to the person accountable for the quality of the decision based on it.

    The three analyses above talk about different things — roles, work breakdown, the line of intervention — but they meet in one place. Wherever an agent appears, you have to answer in advance a question that used to be settled along the way: whose decision is this. In the responsibility matrix it is one Accountable, in the WBS it is written-down boundaries and approval points, in the complexity field it is the choice of mode. Three different mechanisms for one thing.

    Tools get cheaper; accountability does not. The more routine automation takes over, the more each judgment left to a person is worth, and the harder it is to see the moment when judgment was replaced by a tool’s output. The marks on this page are an attempt to make that moment visible, at least in the text: the rail on the left says where each block came from and does not let you pass off someone else’s fact as your own conclusion, or your own guess as verified.
