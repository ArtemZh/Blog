② Milestones · baseline vs current forecast
What it is for. To show schedule drift honestly: not «are we late today», but how far the current forecast has moved away from the date we committed to.
How to read it. Each milestone has two dates — the baseline and the current forecast. The gap between them is the drift; a gap that grows from milestone to milestone means the delay is accumulating rather than being absorbed.
③ Earned Value Management
What it is for. For fixed-price work, to answer «is the money spent matched by work actually done» — a burn rate alone cannot tell overspend from being ahead of plan. For T&M the same card shows a burn-up against the budget ceiling instead, because earned value on T&M is a simulation of control, not control.
How to read it. PV is what we planned to have earned by now, EV what we have earned, AC what it cost. EV below PV — behind schedule; AC above EV — over cost. CPI and SPI below 1.0 say the same in one number; EAC is where the project lands if nothing changes.

⑨ Finance · project P&L · internal
Month by month · plan vs actual hours, cost, revenue, margin
What it is for. The tiles above give the project's end state; this is where the money is actually made or lost — month by month, with hours next to it, because margin moves through hours before it shows up in the P&L.
How to read it. Solid bars are closed months, pale bars are forecast — the split is the current month. The line is margin against the target. Hours under plan with revenue on plan means the team is not fully used; hours over plan with revenue flat means we are paying for work nobody is billing.

Share of the team, grouped by PMBOK domain — the same cut as the audit workbook
What it is for. To see how many people sit in the red on each question, not what the question scores on average.
How to read it. One bar is one question, its length split into green / yellow / red shares of the team. A short green segment on a question with a decent average means a few people are pulling it up while the rest are not.
Average score by PMBOK performance domain
Questions regrouped from the team view into the standard's domains
What it is for. To translate the team's own wording into PMBOK domains, so the audit can be compared with other teams and with the next wave.
How to read it. Each axis is a domain; the further from the centre, the better. A dent in one direction points at the domain to work on.
Change since the previous wave
What it is for. An audit is worth something only against the previous one: it shows whether the actions taken last time moved anything.
How to read it. Bars to the right are domains that gained points, to the left — that lost them. A domain we acted on and that did not move means the action did not work.
Average against spread
Left half — low scores; upper half — the team is split. Bottom-left is a systemic problem, top-left is a disagreement worth a conversation
What it is for. To separate two different problems that the average alone cannot tell apart: everybody is unhappy, or the team disagrees.
How to read it. One dot is one question: horizontally its average, vertically the spread between the highest and the lowest answer. Bottom-left — bad and agreed, fix the process. Top-left — bad and disputed, the answer is in a conversation, not in a process.
Health indicator · one dot per interview
The average hides the split: these are the individual answers
What it is for. One number for «how are you doing» per person, kept anonymous but not averaged away.
How to read it. Every dot is one interview placed on a 0–10 scale. Dots bunched together mean the team is in one state; dots at both ends mean two teams in one.
Red answers by domain
What it is for. To rank domains by the number of answers below 5 — the raw count of unhappy answers, not their average.
How to read it. The longest bar is where the red answers pile up. Compare it with the radar above: a domain with a decent average and many reds is a split, not a healthy domain.
② Clusters · who answers alike and which questions move together
What it is for. An average says the team scores 7; it does not say that four people put 9 and three put 3. Clustering puts people who answer alike next to each other, so a split shows up as a block of colour instead of hiding inside the mean.
How to read it. One column is one interview, one row is one question. Columns are grouped into A / B / C by how similar the answers are; rows are grouped into bands by which questions move together. Look for a vertical block of dark red — that is a group of people who are unhappy across the board — and for a horizontal band of red — a question the whole team rates low. Where the two cross is the first conversation to have.
③ Where the team agrees and where it does not
Average is only half the answer: a wide spread means the team is split, and that is where the problem sits
What it is for. The same questions as a list you can walk through in a team meeting, ordered so the disputed ones come first.
How to read it. The bar shows the range from the lowest to the highest answer, the mark on it is the average. A long bar means the same working week is experienced very differently by different people — ask about it before changing anything.
